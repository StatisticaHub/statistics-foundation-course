## Learning objectives

By the end of this note, you should be able to:

- explain why raw data need to be organised;
- construct and interpret frequency tables;
- distinguish frequency, relative frequency, and percentage;
- choose suitable graphs for categorical and quantitative variables;
- explain the difference between bar charts and histograms;
- describe centre, spread, shape, and outliers from graphs;
- avoid common mistakes in graphical summaries.

## 1. Introduction

Raw data are often difficult to interpret.

A dataset may contain hundreds or thousands of values. Without organisation, it is hard to see patterns.

Tables and graphs help us move from raw observations to visible structure.

The goal is not only to make data look attractive. The goal is to understand what the data are saying.

A good summary helps answer questions such as:

- Which values are common?
- Which values are unusual?
- How spread out are the observations?
- Is the distribution symmetric or skewed?
- Are there gaps or clusters?
- Are there differences between groups?

:::note[Core idea]

Before applying formulas, first understand the structure of the data.

:::

## 2. Raw data

Suppose we observe:

$$
x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n.
$$

These are raw observations.

If $n$ is small, we may inspect the values directly. But if $n$ is large, raw data become difficult to understand.

For example, a list of 500 exam marks is less useful than a table or graph showing how marks are distributed.

## 3. Frequency tables

A **frequency table** shows how often each category or value occurs.

For a categorical variable, the table usually contains:

- categories;
- frequencies;
- relative frequencies;
- percentages.

If category $A$ appears $f_A$ times in a sample of size $n$, then the relative frequency is:

$$
\frac{f_A}{n}.
$$

The percentage is:

$$
\frac{f_A}{n}\times 100.
$$

## 4. Example of a frequency table

Suppose 40 students are asked for their favourite subject.

| Subject | Frequency | Relative frequency | Percentage |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Mathematics | 12 | 0.30 | 30% |
| Science | 10 | 0.25 | 25% |
| English | 8 | 0.20 | 20% |
| History | 6 | 0.15 | 15% |
| Art | 4 | 0.10 | 10% |

Here:

$$
n=40.
$$

For Mathematics:

$$
\frac{12}{40}=0.30.
$$

So the percentage is:

$$
0.30\times 100=30\%.
$$

## 5. Bar charts

A **bar chart** is used for categorical variables.

Each bar represents a category. The height of the bar represents frequency, relative frequency, or percentage.

Bar charts are useful for variables such as:

- blood group;
- gender category;
- treatment group;
- disease status;
- favourite subject.

The bars are separated because the categories are distinct.

:::tip[Interpretation]

For a bar chart, ask which category is most common, which is least common, and whether the distribution is balanced.

:::

## 6. Histograms

A **histogram** is used for quantitative data.

It groups numerical values into intervals called bins.

For example, exam marks may be grouped as:

$$
0\text{-}10,\quad 10\text{-}20,\quad 20\text{-}30,\quad \ldots,\quad 90\text{-}100.
$$

A histogram helps us see the distribution of a numerical variable.

It can reveal:

- centre;
- spread;
- skewness;
- outliers;
- multiple peaks;
- gaps.

## 7. Bar chart versus histogram

| Feature | Bar chart | Histogram |
|---|---|---|
| Data type | Categorical | Quantitative |
| Bars separated? | Usually yes | Usually no |
| Order important? | Sometimes | Yes, numerical order |
| Main use | Compare categories | Show distribution |

A common mistake is using a histogram for categorical data or a bar chart style for continuous data without understanding the distinction.

## 8. Shape of a distribution

Graphs help us describe the shape of data.

A distribution may be:

- symmetric;
- right-skewed;
- left-skewed;
- unimodal;
- bimodal;
- approximately uniform.

A graph also helps identify outliers.

An outlier is an observation that appears unusually far from the rest of the data.

## 9. Common mistakes

:::caution[Common mistakes]

Do not use graphs only as decoration.

Do not choose a graph without considering the variable type.

Do not hide important patterns by using poor intervals or unclear labels.

Do not interpret a graph without checking the scale.

:::

## 10. Summary

Tables and graphs are the first tools of statistical understanding.

They help us move from:

$$
\text{raw observations}
\quad \longrightarrow \quad
\text{visible patterns}.
$$

A good summary should match the variable type and the research question.
