## Why summaries are needed

Raw data are individual observations.

For example:

$$
x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n.
$$

When $n$ is small, we may inspect the values directly. But when $n$ is large, raw values become difficult to interpret.

Summaries help us ask:

- What values occur most often?
- Are the values spread out?
- Are there unusual observations?
- Is the distribution symmetric or skewed?
- Are there differences between groups?

## Frequency tables

A frequency table shows how often each category or value occurs.

If category $A$ appears $f_A$ times in a sample of size $n$, then the relative frequency is:

$$
\frac{f_A}{n}.
$$

The percentage is:

$$
\frac{f_A}{n}\times 100.
$$

**Emma:** Frequency is the count, relative frequency is the proportion, and percentage is the proportion out of 100.

**Mr. R:** Exactly.

## Bar charts

A bar chart is useful for categorical variables.

Each bar represents a category, and the height of the bar represents frequency or percentage.

**James:** Why are the bars separated?

**Mr. R:** Because categories are distinct. The gaps remind us that the categories are separate.

## Histograms

A histogram is useful for quantitative data.

A histogram groups numerical values into intervals.

For example, marks may be grouped as:

$$
0\text{-}10,\quad 10\text{-}20,\quad 20\text{-}30,\quad \ldots,\quad 90\text{-}100.
$$

The height of each bar shows how many observations fall in that interval.

## Mini conversation: bar chart or histogram?

**Oliver:** If I have blood group data, should I use a histogram?

**Mr. R:** No. Blood group is categorical, so a bar chart is more appropriate.

**Emma:** If I have height data, I can use a histogram.

**Mr. R:** Correct.

## What graphs help us see

Graphs help us identify:

- centre;
- spread;
- shape;
- skewness;
- clusters;
- gaps;
- outliers.

:::tip[Key intuition]

Graphs are not just for presentation. They help us think statistically before calculating.

:::

## Common mistake

:::caution[Common mistake]

Do not use the same graph for every variable.

Categorical variables and quantitative variables need different summaries.

:::

## Key takeaway

Tables and graphs help us move from:

$$
\text{raw data}
\quad \longrightarrow \quad
\text{visible structure}.
$$
